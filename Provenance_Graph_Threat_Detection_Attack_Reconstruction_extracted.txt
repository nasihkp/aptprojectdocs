a b s t r a c t
Advanced persistent threat (APT) attacks are sophisticated, stealthy, and persistent, posing signiﬁcant challenges 
to timely detection and investigation in modern network environments. Provenance graph analysis has become 
an important method for APT detection due to its ability to capture detailed causal relationships among system 
entities. However, existing methods suﬀer from several limitations: (1) lack of labeled attack data, (2) lack of 
high-level semantics in attack scenario reconstruction, and (3) high computational overhead limiting practical 
deployment. In this paper, we propose MGDA, a self-supervised method for eﬀective and accurate threat detection 
as well as interpretable attack scenario reconstruction. MGDA introduces a multi-view masked graph autoencoder 
that jointly captures deep semantic features and structural patterns, enabling accurate detection of stealthy and 
unknown attacks. In the reconstruction phase, MGDA combines contextual analysis with rule-based attack pattern 
matching to produce attack scenario graphs that incorporate high-level semantics. We evaluate MGDA on three 
widely used datasets, including both real-world and simulated network attacks. The results demonstrate that 
MGDA achieves an average precision of 97.58 % and F1-score of 98.03 % in threat detection, outperforming 
state-of-the-art approaches. In addition, the automatically reconstructed scenario graphs help identify potential 
multi-step attacks and their stages, aiding analysts in conducting eﬃcient network attack investigations.
1.  Introduction
Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs) are sophisticated and persistent 
attacks that leverage a variety of techniques to conduct highly stealthy, 
multi-stage, long-duration network attacks on the target. As one of the 
most severe threats to modern network security, APTs typically target 
critical hosts in large enterprises and government infrastructures. At-
tackers often emulate trusted benign processes to hide their attacks and 
use novel techniques to evade detection and prolong their presence on 
the target system. They are diﬃcult to detect using traditional detec-
tion methods. Statistically, malicious behaviors are deeply buried within 
massive volumes of benign system events, with attack data sometimes 
accounting for less than 0.001 % of the total audit data volume. Such an 
extremely low percentage makes it diﬃcult for traditional approaches 
based on anomaly detection to function eﬀectively. In addition, insuﬃ-
cient modeling of system behaviors may cause APT activities to closely 
resemble normal behavior in the feature space, thereby reducing the 
discriminative power of detection methods. Existing intrusion detection

rected graph, called a provenance graph, where nodes represent subjects 
(e.g., processes) and objects (e.g., ﬁles and network sockets) and edges 
denote the interactions between these entities. The provenance graph 
preserves the causal relationship between system activities, which can 
eﬀectively analyze the cause of threat events, attack paths, and their 
impacts, providing high detection eﬃciency and robustness for threat 
detection and investigation [2].
APT detection based on provenance graphs can be categorized into 
the following three types. The ﬁrst is rule-based detection [3–7], which 
discovers attacks through rule matching. While eﬀective in capturing 
known attack patterns, these methods incur high manual costs in rule en-
gineering and maintenance, lack adaptability to novel threats, and often 
result in a high false positive rate, contributing to alert fatigue and de-
creased operational eﬃciency. Statistical-based approaches [8,9] iden-

tify system entities, interactions, and communities by analyzing their 
rarity and anomaly scores. However, this approach is limited to shallow 
feature (such as node in-degree and out-degree) extraction from prove-
nance graphs, making it diﬃcult to eﬀectively extract the rich contextual 
information hidden between nodes. As a result, the ﬁne-grained seman-
tic associations between system entities and behaviors cannot be well 
modeled. This often leads to high false positive rates when dealing with 
complex or stealthy attacks. Learning-based approaches [10,11], such 
as machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) models, are currently 
popular due to their ability to automatically learn behavior patterns 
from data.
After detection, existing approaches often attempt to reconstruct at-
tack scenarios by correlating anomalous activities based on simple tem-
poral relations. For example, Kairos [12] applies community detection 
and aggregation techniques to group provenance data into meaningful 
subgraphs, while Flash [13] constructs attack evolution graphs through 
causal tracing to link diﬀerent attack stages. These methods help an-
alysts organize massive event data into more manageable structures. 
However, they only address the symptoms rather than the root cause: 
the reconstructed graphs are often oversized and redundant, and more 
critically, they lack the abstraction of higher-level semantics such as 
attack techniques and stages. Without such semantics, it is diﬃcult to 
connect ﬁne-grained system events to broader multi-stage campaigns, 
which signiﬁcantly limits the interpretability and eﬀectiveness of attack 
scenario reconstruction.
While promising, these methods still face signiﬁcant limitations 
when applied to real-world attack detection and investigation. The prac-
tical application of such approaches remains challenging due to the fol-
lowing key issues: (1) Lack of labeled attack data. Most learning-based 
methods rely on supervised training and require suﬃcient labeled attack 
samples. In real-world settings, however, attack data is scarce, and man-
ual labeling is expensive and diﬃcult to scale, which limits the model’s 
ability to detect unknown or novel attacks. (2) Lack of high-level se-
mantics in attack scenario reconstruction. Existing methods often re-
construct attack scenarios by linking low-level system events based on 
simple correlations (e.g., timestamps). This approach produces overly 
large and redundant graphs and fails to capture higher-level semantics, 
such as attack stages and techniques, which are essential for understand-
ing multi-stage attacks. As a result, the reconstructed scenarios are often 
incomplete and less interpretable, increasing both the cost and diﬃculty 
of attack investigation. Moreover, existing works frequently struggle to 
balance the accuracy of attack detection with the completeness and in-
terpretability of the reconstructed scenarios. (3) High computational 
overhead. The scale and complexity of system audit logs are rapidly in-
creasing, resulting in massive and often redundant provenance graphs. 
Some DL methods are impractical for real-world deployment due to high 
time and memory overhead, making it diﬃcult to eﬃciently analyze the 
provenance graph and perform real-time threat detection.
To address these challenges, we propose MGDA, a self-supervised 
method for APT detection and attack scenario reconstruction. MGDA 
leverages multi-view masked graph representation learning and outlier 
detection to identify malicious entities from provenance graphs. It then 
applies contextual analysis and rule-based matching to reconstruct at-
tack scenarios. MGDA ﬁrst constructs the provenance graph from au-
dit logs. It then employs a graph representation module to perform 
both feature and structure reconstruction in a self-supervised manner. 
A lightweight anomaly detection module is applied to identify suspi-
cious entities. Finally, based on the detected anomalous nodes, MGDA 
performs contextual analysis and rule-based matching of tactics, tech-
niques, and procedures (TTPs) to reconstruct attack scenarios with rich 
semantics such as attack stages. Our approach not only accurately de-
tects malicious entities, but also reconstructs interpretable attack sce-
narios that facilitate analysts’ understanding and investigation.
We performed a comprehensive evaluation of MGDA using datasets 
widely adopted by the research community, including StreamSpot [14], 
Unicorn Wget [10], and the DARPA Transparent Computing E3 datasets 
[15]. These datasets encompass a broad spectrum of attack scenarios 
and system behaviors. Evaluation results show that MGDA achieves an 
average precision of 97.58 % and an F1-score of 98.03 %. Furthermore, 
MGDA maintains low computational overhead and demonstrates up to 
3× faster training speed compared to existing methods, highlighting its 
practicality in real-world deployment scenarios.
The primary contributions of our work are as follows:
• We propose MGDA, a self-supervised framework that tightly inte-
grates anomaly detection and attack investigation. It models benign 
behaviors to reduce reliance on labeled attack data and eﬀectively 
detect stealthy or previously unseen attacks.
• We employ a novel multi-view masked graph auto-encoder to cap-
ture deep hidden semantics among system entities, combined with 
a sample-based structure reconstruction strategy to learn structural 
patterns. These designs jointly produce high-quality embeddings, im-
proving detection accuracy.
• We propose a TTP rule-based attack scenario reconstruction method 
inspired by the ATT&CK framework. It can generate attack scenario 
graphs with high-level contextual semantics, eﬀectively supporting 
the tracking and analysis of complex attacks.
• We conduct a comprehensive evaluation of MGDA on three widely 
used datasets. Results demonstrate that MGDA outperforms most ex-
isting methods in APT detection while maintaining low computa-
tional overhead.
2.  Related work
MGDA is mainly related to two major research areas: APT detection 
and attack investigation.
2.1.  APT detection
Rule-based approaches rely on analyzing historical attacks to con-
struct predeﬁned rules, which are then used to match malicious behav-
iors for APT detection. For instance, Holmes [4] designs a high-level 
scenario graph (HSG) by leveraging threat subgraphs constructed by se-
curity experts as knowledge graphs. It deﬁnes 16 types of TTP rules 
across seven APT attack phases, mapping low-level system activities 
to the MITRE ATT&CK matrix [16]. These rules are matched to sys-
tem behaviors in provenance graphs via graph matching algorithms, 
enabling the identiﬁcation of attack phases. The method relies entirely 
on expert-deﬁned rules, and the detection results are heavily depen-
dent on security experts. RapSheet [3] integrates detection rules from a 
commercial Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR) to map attacks to 
ATT&CK-related tactics, which covers more speciﬁc information about 
the attacking techniques and tactics compared to Holmes. Sleuth [5] 
and Morse [7] utilize label-based rule deﬁnitions to trigger alerts. How-
ever, they suﬀer from label over-propagation, where long-term attacks 
cause exponential growth in node dependencies, leading to dependency 
explosion. Poirot [6] manually constructs query graphs from APT at-
tack reports, and uses fuzzy graph alignment techniques for detection. 
However, its computational complexity grows rapidly with the graph 
size, limiting its real-time applicability. Overall, the rule engineering 
and maintenance process for these heuristic methods is costly and labor-
intensive, requiring constant updating of the knowledge base and rules. 
Moreover, since APT attacks often leverage zero-day exploits, rule-based 
approaches struggle to detect novel or unknown threats, keeping defend-
ers perpetually behind advanced adversaries.
Statistical-based methods [8,17,18] quantify the rarity of statistical 
entities or behaviors within the provenance graph, assigning suspicion 
scores to these entities, which ultimately leads to anomaly detection. Pri-
otracker [18] and Nodoze [8] adopt statistical feature-based attack de-
tection methods by prioritizing anomalous events and causal dependen-
cies. PrioTracker assigns higher priority to edges of anomalous events. 
NoDoze improves on the above by prioritizing entire paths by rarity.

Alerts are initiated for rare paths. These approaches assume that the 
processes involved in an attack exhibit anomalous behavior. However, 
they often fail when malicious activities are carefully blended into be-

statistical approaches typically extract only shallow features from the 
provenance graph and often fail to capture the rich semantic relation-
ships among entities, resulting in elevated false positive rates.
Learning-based methods detect APT attacks by training models to 
recognize activities that are diﬀerent from normal behavior. These meth-
ods often leverage anomaly detection techniques to uncover novel or 
previously unseen attacks by evaluating deviations in runtime behaviors 
from established benign execution proﬁles. Graph-based anomaly detec-
tion approaches [10,14] utilize graph kernel techniques to dynamically 
model system behavior and identify anomalous graphs via clustering-
based analysis. Threatrace [19] detects host-based threats in real-time at 
the granularity of system entities without prior knowledge of attack pat-
terns. It customizes GraphSAGE, an inductive graph neural network, to 
learn role embeddings of benign entities and detect anomalies by iden-
tifying deviations from predicted roles. MAGIC [20] employs a masked 
graph autoencoder to model the behavior of benign system entities. The 
model is trained by reconstructing node feature masks and structural 
features in the graph, and subsequently applies a KNN-based outlier 
detector to identify anomalous system behaviors. However, the perfor-
mance of this method is aﬀected by the quality of the training data, 
and many false alarms may be generated if low quality data is used for 
training. KAIROS [12] adopts an encoder-decoder graph neural network 
to capture the temporal evolution of structural changes in provenance 
graphs, enabling ﬁne-grained assessment of system event anomalies. 
Leveraging this ﬁne-grained information, KAIROS reconstructs attack 
traces and generates compact summary graphs that accurately charac-
terize malicious activities in the system audit log stream. Flash [13] uti-
lizes Word2Vec-based semantic encoding to capture entities and tem-
poral sequences, encodes local and global graph structures into node 
embeddings. A lightweight classiﬁer with GNN and Word2Vec embed-
dings learns benign node behavior and detects anomalies in real-time.
2.2.  Attack investigation
Attack investigation techniques can provide important support to 
security analysts, not only by helping to verify the validity of alerts, 
but also by assisting in the reconstruction and interpretation of threat 
events. Some detection methods can indirectly realize the reconstruc-
tion of attack scenarios during deployment. Recent studies [21] attempt 
to bridge the gap between detection and investigation by reconstruct-
ing attack scenarios, improving both the eﬃciency and interpretability 
of investigation. APTSHIELD [22] integrates ATT&CK to set entity tags 
and propagation rules, enabling alert aggregation and attack path re-
covery via label propagation. Holmes [4] utilizes TTP-based speciﬁca-
tions to visualize high-level attack scenarios and relate suspicious ﬂows 
in audit logs. Other approaches such as RapSheet [3] leverage Tactical 
Provenance Graphs (TPGs) to oﬀer concise, multi-stage attack visualiza-
tions, supporting faster investigation and alert generation. Flash [13] 
constructs Attack Evolution Graphs (AEGs) by tracing causal relation-
ships, helping analysts associate the various stages of an APT attack ef-
ﬁciently. ATLAS [11] relies on the performance of model learning and 
has low interpretability in reconstructing attack graphs. Krystal [23] 
constructs attack graph from provenance graphs by correlating alerts 
through forward and backward search. In addition, they construct at-
tack query graphs based on external knowledge such as Cyber Threat 
Intelligence (CTI) reports, public malware documents, etc. It can further 
use graph queries to identify high-level attack stages in the generated 
attack graphs, providing richer information to analysts.
The core goal of attack investigation is to mitigate alert fatigue 
and enable analysts to rapidly obtain a holistic understanding of the 
threat. Therefore, accuracy, timeliness, and interpretability of investiga-

existing approaches struggle to strike a good balance among these as-
pects. In contrast, our approach generates attack scenario graphs that

3.1.  Motivating example
This is an attack case from the DARPA E3 CADETS dataset. The at-
tacker exploits a vulnerable Nginx server on the CADETS FreeBSD host 
to gain initial access. A shell connection is then established from IP ad-
dress 81.49.200.166. Through this shell, the attacker downloads a mali-
cious payload to /tmp/vUgefal and executes it with root privileges. The 
vUgefal process then moves laterally to 61.167.39.128, completes the in-
trusion, and attempts to inject into the sshd process while downloading 
a second payload to /var/log/devc. Fig. 1 illustrates our motivating ex-
ample. Three types of nodes represent system entities—processes, ﬁles, 
and network sockets—while directed edges indicate their interactions.
3.2.  Threat model
To build a comprehensive threat model, we make the following as-
sumptions regarding attacker behavior and system characteristics. We 
focus on APTs that inﬁltrate target hosts via network channels, pre-
installed backdoors, or software vulnerabilities. These attacks are typi-
cally highly stealthy and can persist over extended periods. During intru-
sion, attackers often conceal malicious behavior within legitimate sys-
tem activities, making it diﬃcult for defenders to distinguish between 
benign and malicious actions. In addition, attackers may exploit zero-
day vulnerabilities, where no prior knowledge or labeled data is avail-
able, posing a signiﬁcant challenge for detection models. They may also 
adopt living-oﬀ-the-land techniques by injecting malicious code into le-
gitimate processes that subsequently spawn new processes to continue 
malicious operations.
Despite the stealthy nature of these threats, system audit logs provide 
a complete and traceable record of activities and evidence. Following 
most prior work in this ﬁeld [10,13,19], we assume that the underlying 
operating system, audit framework, and hardware are trustworthy [24]. 
We further assume that attackers cannot tamper with audit logs, pre-
serving the semantic and structural integrity of the provenance graphs 
constructed by MGDA.

4.1.  Provenance graph construction
MGDA collects kernel-level audit logs from various sources (e.g., 
Windows ETW, Linux Audit, and CamFlow [24]) and transforms them 
uniformly into a graph structure known as a Provenance Graph (PG). 
The logs are parsed into structured records. Each log entry (subject, re-
lation, object, timestamp) describes an interaction between system en-
tities, where the subject and object denote the participating entities and 
the relation speciﬁes the type of interaction. Based on these interactions, 
MGDA constructs a directed provenance graph, in which nodes repre-
sent system entities and edges represent their interactions. The direction 
of each edge reﬂects the ﬂow of information. For example, when process 
A reads ﬁle B, an edge is created from node A to node B (A → R-read 
→ B). Table 1 summarizes the entity and relation types considered in 
MGDA. Fig. 1 shows the provenance graph of a real-world APT attack. 
MGDA considers three types of information: node types (e.g., pro-
cess, ﬁle), edge types (e.g., read, execute), and semantic attributes of 
nodes (e.g., process paths, ﬁle names, command-line arguments, source 
IP addresses). During model training, only node and edge types are used 
for structural feature embedding to control training complexity. Mean-
while, semantic attributes are stored in a mapping dictionary indexed 
by node IDs. This design reduces the graph structure training complex-
ity while retaining rich semantic information to support interpretable 
and context-aware attack scenario reconstruction.
Data compression. Data compression can reduce resource consump-
tion and alleviate cache pressure. However, it is crucial to preserve the 
data dependency during compression to ensure the integrity and trace-
ability of the results. To address this, we adopt a semantics-preserving 
compression strategy [25,26] that eliminates redundant events in audit 
logs. Events are merged only when they are semantically equivalent, 
meaning the state of the source entity remains unchanged and the desti-
nation entity receives multiple identical information ﬂows. For example, 
as illustrated in Fig. 3, events t2 and t4 both correspond to process1 read 
ﬁle1 at diﬀerent timestamps. Only the earliest system event (t2) is re-
tained. This compression reduces graph size and redundancy without 
losing essential behavior semantics. It improves training eﬃciency and 
eliminates repeated actions. Moreover, it enables the model to focus 
Fig. 3. An example of data compression.
on diverse and representative benign behaviors, rather than repeated 
instances of the same action, thereby enhancing threat detection perfor-
mance.
Initial embedding. We initialize the embeddings of nodes and 
edges during the graph construction stage. Unlike prior approaches 
such as Flash [13] that incorporate semantic attribute encoding us-
ing Word2Vec, our approach adopts a simpliﬁed yet eﬀective encoding 
strategy based solely on the one-hot representation of node and edge 
types. Speciﬁcally, each node is encoded by its type (e.g., process, ﬁle, 
socket), and each edge is encoded based on its interaction type (e.g., 
read, write, execute), thereby enabling lightweight modeling of system 
interactions. For instance, given ﬁve types of edge relations, each edge 
is represented by a 5-dimensional one-hot vector, where [0, 0, 0, 0, 1] 
denotes the ﬁfth type and [1, 0, 0, 0, 0] denotes the ﬁrst.
We empirically ﬁnd that incorporating semantic attributes of nodes 
(such as process paths, ﬁle names, or command-line parameters) 
through hash-based [12] embeddings signiﬁcantly increases the com-
plexity of subsequent representation learning and introduces noise, 
which in turn harms the model’s anomaly detection accuracy. Therefore, 
we discard semantic attributes during the feature embedding phase and 
instead preserve them externally for downstream tasks such as attack 
scenario reconstruction and analyst interpretation.
4.2.  Graph representation
To address the scarcity of labeled attack data in real-world envi-
ronments, we employ a graph self-supervised learning (SSL) technique 
to derive high-quality embeddings from the original feature graph. 
Existing masked self-encoders such as GraphMAE [27] have shown

promising results in reconstructing node features. However, their per-
formance heavily depends on the distinguishability of input features and 
is vulnerable to feature interference. In APT scenarios, malicious nodes 
often mimic benign behavior through a large number of normal-like 
operations, obscuring their malicious intent. This behavior reduces the 
feature distinction between benign and malicious nodes, ultimately de-
grading the robustness and detection accuracy of the model.
To enhance embedding quality and model robustness, We propose 
an improved graph representation learning model inspired by Magic 
[20] and GraphMAE2 [28]. The node feature reconstruction is regu-
larized using multi-view random mask decoding as well as introducing 
a latent representation prediction strategy that performs reconstruction 
in the embedding space. In addition, graph structure reconstruction is 
achieved by the edge connection prediction task, which strengthens the 
model’s ability to capture global topological relationships. The combina-
tion of multi-view masking and latent representation learning improves 
model resilience to noise and enables the capture of implicit semantics 
and structural patterns among system entities. As a result, the model 
generates higher quality and more discriminative node embeddings, lay-
ing a solid foundation for downstream tasks such as node classiﬁcation 
and anomaly detection. The graph representation module consists of 
three components: node masking, graph encoder, and graph decoder. 
The encoder and decoder form a masked graph autoencoder.
4.2.1.  Node masking
During training, the graph representation module applies masking 
operations on the nodes in the graph. Edges are not masked because 
they provide critical relational dependencies among system entities. The 
masked nodes are obtained by random sampling, similar to the pre-
training process of MAE or BERT [29]. Randomly sample some nodes 
from the graph and replace their corresponding features with mask to-
ken [MASK] or all-zero vectors. A relatively high mask ratio (e.g., 50 %) 
is employed for sampling, which helps to eliminate redundancy and 
learn robust structural and contextual representations.

4.2.2.  Graph encoder
The initial node embeddings obtained from the graph construction 
phase are based on primitive features only. However, these primitive 
features often fail to capture the complex behavior of system entities. 
Contextual information about system entities, such as their neighbor-
ing nodes, multi-hop relationships, and interaction patterns with other 
entities, is crucial for high-quality node embedding generation.
To this end, we adopt and extend the Graph Masked Auto-Encoder 
GraphMAE2 [28] to generate more semantic node representations in a 
self-supervised manner. The graph masked auto-encoder consists of an 
encoder and a decoder: the encoder generates context-aware embed-
dings by propagating and aggregating node features over the graph; the 
decoder attempts to reconstruct the original features to provide a super-
vised signal for training. The decoder performs the following three tasks: 
1) Generate multiple corrupted codes through randomly re-masking the 
code, which are all forced to reconstruct the input features after GNN 
decoding. 2) An MLP-based decoder predicts latent target representa-
tions, which are generated by the target generator and the unmasked 
graph. 3) Meanwhile, reconstruct the structure by predicting the edges 
between nodes.
Empirical results from GraphMAE and GraphMAE2 show that the 
Graph Attention Network (GAT) [30] achieves superior embedding and 
outperforms other methods on node classiﬁcation tasks. Therefore, we 
utilize a multi-layer stacked GAT as the encoder to obtain a more ex-
pressive node representation. GAT improves the ability to model inter-
node relationships by introducing an attention mechanism that assigns 
learnable weights to diﬀerent neighboring nodes, enabling the model 
to pay more attention to important nodes when aggregating neighbor-
ing features. By stacking multiple layers of GAT, the encoder captures 
deep graph structure information and multi-hop interaction patterns.

shallow associations, lacking the capacity to model deeper semantic 
structures and contextual dependencies. As a result, the learned repre-

address these limitations and mitigate overﬁtting in feature reconstruc-
tion, we introduce two regularization strategies during decoding: multi-
view random re-mask decoding and latent representation prediction. 
In addition, considering that provenance graph contains extensive en-
tity interactions that reﬂects the semantic associations and behavioral 
structure of the system under normal operation, we further introduce 
a structure reconstruction task to enhance the model’s ability to learn 
graph structures. The decoder consists of the following three core com-

4.3.  Threat detection
After obtaining the output embedding from the graph representa-
tion module, we employ a lightweight unsupervised anomaly detection 
module. Its core idea is to identify potentially malicious nodes or system 
states by modeling the degree of deviation of the sample distribution in 
the embedding space using the K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) method.
The detection module does not require a complex learning process 
in the training phase, but only needs to memorize the embeddings of 
benign samples and organize them into a K-d tree index structure to 
accelerate subsequent queries. After training, the module performs the 
following three steps to realize outlier detection:
1) K-nearest neighbor search: For each embedding representation of 
a test sample, ﬁrst retrieve its K-nearest neighbors from the K-D tree 
constructed over the training embedding.
2) Similarity calculation: Compute the average distance between the 
test embedding and its K-neighbors. To make this distance compara-
ble across diﬀerent regions of the embedding space, normalize it by 
the average neighbor distance observed for the corresponding neigh-
bors in the training set. The resulting normalized value is used as the 
anomaly (outlier) score.
3) Outlier ﬁltering: If this outlier score exceeds a predeﬁned threshold 
𝜃, the corresponding system entity or state is deemed anomalous and 
ﬂagged as a potential threat. This detection module can be adapted 
to diﬀerent granularity of detection tasks: batch-level detection and 
entity-level detection.

paths, thus increasing the burden on security analysts to perform man-

MGL requires over 45 min. MGDA achieves nearly 100× speedup over 
ShadeWatcher and more than 3× faster runtime than APT-MGL.
Moreover, the E3-Trace sub-dataset is collected on a two-week cy-
cle, generating about 1.37 GB of audit logs daily. Under this workload, 
MGDA requires only about 2 min per day to complete APT detection us-
ing CPU resources alone. This high eﬃciency not only veriﬁes the prac-
ticality of MGDA in real deployment environments, but also highlights 
its strong real-time detection capability under resource-constrained con-
ditions.
5.5.  Reconstruction performance (RQ4)
We empirically analyze the attack scenario reconstruction capabil-
ity using the Cadets sub-dataset as a case study. Fig. 6 below shows the 
attack scenario reconstruction done with the anomalous nodes detected 
by the model. Based on the anomalous nodes detected by the system, 
including process and ﬁle alerts such as sshd, /tmp/font, XIM, test, etc. 
We perform forward-backward event correlation on the alerted nodes, 
combined with the cropping and event sifting of the intermediate nodes, 
to construct a compact and representative suspicious subgraph. Then we 
combine the TTP rule design ideas in Homles to automatically associate 
high-risk operations with MITRE ATT&CK techniques and tactics, real-
izing the precise annotation of attack techniques and stages. In the gen-
erated attack scenario reconstruction diagram, it can be seen that the 
attacker performs multiple attack operations such as ﬁle creation, ﬁle 
execution, data leakage, and so on. The reconstructed attack scenario re-
veals the kill chain stages of the attack, such as the four stages of recon-
naissance, defense evasion, execution, and penetration. It is worth not-
ing that through the correlation relationship of the nodes in the graph, 
analysts can further manually screen and correct false positives. For ex-
ample, in addition to the attack behaviors mentioned in the oﬃcial at-

as reading etc/passwd. Although ground truth does not mark this node 
as an attack node, our attack graph reveals its important role in the at-
tack path, suggesting that the attack graph structure helps to discover 
more potential attack links. We manually compared and calibrated the 
system’s reconstructed CADETS attack process with the dataset’s pub-
licly available oﬃcial attack reports. The results show that the recon-
structed attack graph not only highly covers the main attack nodes and 
events speciﬁed in the report, but also supplements the discovery of 
hidden behaviors such as etc/passwd reading. This improves the com-
prehensiveness and accuracy of attack traceability. The above results 
validate the utility of this method in attack scenario reduction, mali-
cious node identiﬁcation, and contextual semantic complementation to 
increase interpretability. Based on the automatic TTP annotation and at-
tack stage mapping, the security analysis team is able to grasp the whole 
picture of the attack in the shortest possible time, precisely locate the 
attack entry and exit nodes, and take emergency response actions such 
as blocking the outreach malicious IPs in a timely manner, thus signiﬁ-
cantly reducing the consequences of the attack. 
6.  Discussion and limitations
False positivies. While MGDA maintains a low false positive rate in 
most settings, it may still produce elevated false alarms in certain sce-
narios. For example, when the logs collected in the training phase do not 
adequately cover the latest benign behavioral patterns in the system, or 
when the training data is of low quality, noisy and biased, the model may 
misclassify unknown normal behaviors as abnormal. This phenomenon 
can be attributed to concept drift, where the evolving nature of system 
behavior leads to a mismatch between learned and current data distribu-
tions. This is a common challenge in anomaly detection systems, espe-
cially in real-world environments where the system is constantly evolv-
ing and the behavioral patterns are continuously changing. To mitigate 
this, MGDA can incorporate an adaptive mechanism that feeds the false 
alarm samples conﬁrmed by security experts to the model for training 
to adapt to the system changes, thus reducing the probability of false 
alarms. Additionally, a dynamic anomaly threshold adjustment strategy 
can further enhance robustness to environmental changes and sustain 
long-term operational stability.
Adversarial attacks. MGDA shows strong capability in detecting 
covert attacks and identifying escape strategies of attackers that mimic 
benign behaviors, but its robustness against attackers with stronger 
a priori knowledge still deserves further exploration. We assume that 
the attacker cannot directly access the structure and parameters of the 
MGDA model, and do not consider data poisoning attacks for the time 
being. However, the attacker may still launch evasion attacks by in-
direct means, e.g., tampering with audit logs, injecting forged nodes, 
or embedding sub-structures of legitimate process activities into the at-
tacking subgraphs in order to disrupt the graph structure to hide mali-
cious behaviors. It is found that MGDA shows strong resistance against 
attacks. It makes it diﬃcult for adversarial attacks that tamper with 
features and malicious entity structures to be eﬀective. Its robustness 
is mainly attributed to the following design aspects: ﬁrst, the graph 
representation module of MGDA mainly relies on its neighbor infor-
mation for the reconstruction of node features and adjacent structures 
in the functional feature and structure reconstruction phases. Second, 
the introduced multi-view mechanism enhances the model’s ability to 
model semantic associations among nodes, thus improving the recog-
nition of mimicry attacks. Lastly, self-supervised training based on the 
model makes the model more focused on the behavioral patterns, re-
ducing the risk of being interfered or spoofed by speciﬁc labels. There-
fore, for attackers to successfully launch adversarial attacks against 
MGDA, they must possess strong prior knowledge. Furthermore, they 
need to accurately manipulate the complex relationships between graph

7.  Conclusion
We propose MGDA, a self-supervised approach for eﬃcient APT 
detection and interpretable attack scenario reconstruction. MGDA 
constructs provenance graphs by encoding nodes and edges with 
lightweight semantics. It models benign system behaviors through 
multi-view masked graph representation learning, enabling the recon-
struction of both features and structures. An outlier detection method 
is then applied to eﬃciently identify anomalous nodes. Subsequently, 
MGDA performs automated correlation analysis and matches attack 
behaviors with tactics and techniques deﬁned in the MITRE ATT&CK 
framework via designed TTP rules. This enables the reconstruction of at-
tack scenario graphs enriched with high-level semantic representations, 
such as attack stages. We evaluated MGDA on three widely adopted 
datasets. Results show that MGDA achieves an average precision of 
97.58 % and F1-score of 98.03 %, with up to 3× faster training speed 
compared to existing methods. MGDA not only maintains low compu-
tational overhead and exhibits strong detection capabilities, but also 
assists security analysts in gaining a comprehensive understanding of 
attack processes and conducting eﬃcient traceability analysis.
